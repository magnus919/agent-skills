# External customer and problem discovery

Use before requirements when the uncertainty is whether a consequential customer
problem exists. Reuse stakeholder mapping for interview selection, question patterns
for neutral questions, gap detection for hypothetical promises, and conflict
resolution for competing assumptions. Do not repeat those methods here.

## Start from a bounded decision

Name the next investment, accountable owner, target segment, decision date, and
what evidence could change the decision. Separate user, buyer, payer, and internal
sponsor. An internal sponsor can establish a requirement or constraint, not external
demand. If the user already brings a draft to grill, keep the artifact-first
interview; record external evidence gaps without replacing it with a questionnaire.

1. Describe the job in context: when the problem arises, what outcome the customer
   seeks, current workaround, frequency, consequences, and alternatives including
   doing nothing. Avoid starting from a feature pitch.
2. Ask for the most recent concrete occurrence and its consequence. Record whether
   the account is testimony or independently observed. Seek authorized artifacts
   such as a redacted workflow record; never demand private data as an entry fee.
3. Distinguish evidence classes in the customer-evidence ledger:
   - Stated preference: compliments, survey intent, hypothetical willingness.
   - Reported past behavior: a specific customer account, still attributed testimony.
   - Observed behavior: an authorized trace or observation with source/date/context.
   - Costly commitment: actual money, time, access, or switching effort put at risk;
     record reversibility, incentives, and who committed. A refundable deposit is
     weaker than retained payment; neither alone proves durable value.
   These classes describe evidence, not a universal numeric score. Recency,
   representativeness, alternatives, and confounding affect every class.
4. Keep segments separate. Seek counterexamples, non-adopters, and former users;
   do not average contradictory needs into a fictitious typical customer. Record
   sampling and access limitations rather than claiming market prevalence.
5. Map the consequential uncertainties: problem/value, solution usability,
   feasibility, and business viability. Pain can be severe while delivery cost,
   purchasing authority, or constraints make this opportunity unattractive. Problem
   evidence does not prove a proposed solution works.
6. Propose the smallest falsifying evidence step with an owner and bounded cost/time.
   Route experiments, metrics, product choices, and financial analysis to their
   owners listed in SKILL.md. Any customer contact, data collection, charge, or
   shared-system write needs the user's normal authority and privacy boundaries.
7. Recommend continue, narrow, defer, or stop against the stated decision. Explain
   contradictory evidence, remaining uncertainty, and the limit of the recommendation.
   Do not require arbitrary interview counts or convert repeated enthusiasm into
   a validation threshold. Reopen the decision when new evidence contradicts it.

## Worked distinction

Ten interviewees say they would buy a scheduling tool. Two report last month's
missed appointments; no records or commitments are available. Record ten stated
preferences and two attributed behavior reports. The problem may merit an
observation of scheduling work, but neither demand nor solution efficacy is
validated. If a segment already has an adequate free workaround, preserve that
counterexample and consider narrowing or stopping rather than escalating spend.
