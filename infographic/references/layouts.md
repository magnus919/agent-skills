# Information layouts and visual encodings

Choose the information layout for the relationship; choose the aesthetic afterward.
Do not make every subject a column of interchangeable cards. Start with a small
wireframe and a clear entry point, reading path, and endpoint. Use empty space to
separate groups and proximity to connect labels with their referents.

| Reader task | Starting structure | Semantic risks and adaptations |
|---|---|---|
| Compare options | Aligned columns or repeated rows with common criteria | Unequal panel sizes imply unequal importance; preserve comparable units |
| Follow instructions | Ordered steps with a clear start/end and decision branches | Numbers imply sequence; show branches rather than inventing a single path |
| Understand change over time | Timeline or time-series story | Equal spacing implies equal time unless explicitly categorical |
| Understand how it works | Annotated mechanism, cutaway, or cause/effect diagram | Differentiate material flow, influence, and association in connector labels |
| Navigate a hierarchy | Tree or nested groups | Containment implies membership; allow cross-links when a pure tree is false |
| Understand a place | Map, route, spatial schematic | Use geography only when spatial position matters; mark not-to-scale schematics |
| Understand evidence | One main chart plus supporting annotation/context | Do not scatter incompatible charts simply to fill space |
| Find an answer quickly | Reference grid, checklist, or table | Favor scanning and direct lookup over a forced narrative |
| Understand a repeating process | Loop with explicit transitions | A ring asserts recurrence; do not turn a finite process into a cycle |

Background tracks and enclosing shapes can imply a whole or denominator. Use a
100% track for rates when useful; do not add the same track behind raw counts
unless that reference total has a real meaning and a label.

A hybrid can work when it has a dominant structure and clear transitions. Use
small multiples for repeated comparisons and common scales when comparison is
the task. If space is insufficient, change format or split the story deliberately.

## Encode the analytical task

For precise comparisons, start with aligned bars or dots on a common axis. For
change over continuous time, consider lines; for distributions, select histograms,
boxplots, or individual observations according to audience knowledge and sample.
A scatterplot can show association, not causal proof. Use a table when exact lookup
matters more than pattern recognition. A part-to-whole chart needs a meaningful
whole; categories may overlap and therefore may not be valid slices.

Magnitude encoded by bar length needs a zero baseline. A line chart need not
always begin at zero, but label the scale and avoid implying exaggerated change.
For area encodings, scale area to value, not radius or diameter. Avoid decorative
perspective that changes apparent size. Explain normalization/logarithms, and do
not pair incompatible axes simply because the lines look correlated. A clear
annotation or separate panel often serves better than a dual-axis chart.

Use position, length, area, color, shape, or texture according to the task and
constraints rather than a universal chart ranking. Shape and position can back
up color. Reserve strong emphasis for the main comparison; mute context without
making essential marks illegible. Label directly when practical and keep legends
close enough that readers do not repeatedly search across the page.

## Test the structure before decoration

Read only the title, section headings, and major shapes. Does the intended message
survive? Follow each connector and identify what it claims. Remove accidental
causality, false ordering, meaningless cycles, or decorative scales. This is a
heuristic inspection; reader comprehension requires an actual reader test.
