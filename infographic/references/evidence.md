# Evidence and editorial judgment

## Establish a useful promise

State the reader's question in ordinary language, then propose the answer the
visual can support. “Our city wastes half its water” is a different claim from
“Where household water goes.” Do not force a striking headline onto evidence
that supports only a descriptive account. For contested or high-stakes subjects,
verify current primary sources and retain population, place, and date boundaries.

For a complex request, keep a compact claim ledger:

| Claim or value | Source and exact location | Scope | Transformation | Caveat | Visual use |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Proposed statement | URL/document, page/table, publication/access date | Population, period, unit, denominator | Formula/filter/rounding or none | Uncertainty, missingness, limitations | Headline, chart, annotation |

User-supplied numbers are a source with stated provenance, not independently
verified findings. Label synthetic or illustrative data visibly. When research
is unavailable, retain known supplied facts, remove unsupported comparisons,
and identify which source would resolve the gap. Do not manufacture a citation
or turn an estimate into a fact to fill a blank panel.

## Edit the argument

Separate must-know claims from context and optional detail. Draft a short headline,
a necessary qualifier, supporting evidence, and any next action. Make headings
carry meaning rather than repeating generic category names. A takeaway can be a
question when the evidence is inconclusive. Place decisive caveats adjacent to
the claim; a tiny footer does not repair a misleading headline.

Reduce material by removing repetition and restructuring. Do not shrink type to
preserve every sentence. For extensive required copy, use panels, a companion
page, or a readable reference format and explain the tradeoff. Preserve exact
required wording where it matters; propose a larger format if it cannot fit.

## Verify quantities before styling

- Recompute totals, ratios, percentage changes, and percentage-point differences.
  Moving from 20% to 30% is +10 percentage points and +50% relative, not +10% relative.
- Check whether populations, collection methods, time windows, and denominators
  are comparable. Show the denominator for rates and part-to-whole claims.
- Separate zero from absent/not measured; avoid lines that silently bridge unknown
  periods. Distinguish forecasts, observations, and illustrative scenarios.
- Preserve meaningful uncertainty. Choose intervals and labels appropriate to what
  they represent. Overlapping intervals alone do not settle significance.
- Distinguish association, plausible mechanism, and demonstrated causality.
  Sequential panels and arrows can accidentally strengthen a claim.
- Round consistently with source precision, retaining unrounded values for geometry
  and calculations. Explain rounding if displayed totals do not add exactly.

For a conceptual explainer, identify the boundary of the analogy. A “library” for
computer memory can help orientation without implying that every technical process
works like a human librarian. Illustration may be imaginative; explanatory
relationships still need to be defensible.
