# Drafting and revising customer-centered messages

Read when the audience and offer are sufficiently defined to write or improve copy.
Use `references/customer-centered-messaging.md` for the full workflow and
`templates/customer-message-brief.md` for the deliverable. This reference supplies
writing decisions, not new claims about persuasion or conversion effectiveness.

## 1. Build a message from evidence

Start with a small working map before polishing sentences:

| Input | Writing decision | If missing |
|---|---|---|
| Audience and immediate task | Name who the offer serves and what they are trying to do | Label the audience hypothesis; draft only what the offer supports |
| Current alternative and its relevant limitation | Explain why someone would consider changing | Ask what they use now; do not declare all alternatives inferior |
| Distinct capability or service arrangement | Show the mechanism behind the difference | Use a plain offer description; return differentiation to positioning |
| Customer consequence | Translate capability into a useful outcome | State the observable deliverable, not invented feelings |
| Proof and its limits | Put the evidence beside the claim it supports | Mark the gap; narrow or remove the claim |
| Customer effort and next action | Explain how to start and what follows | Keep terms open until confirmed |

Trace **capability → changed customer activity → relevant outcome**. For example,
“saved approval history” enables “see who approved a change” and can support
“prepare the review trail.” It does not by itself support “pass every audit.”
Stop the chain at the last supported link. Customer quotes can inform language;
a quotation from one person does not establish prevalence across a segment.

## 2. Draft materially different candidates

When comparing approaches, draft two or three alternatives that emphasize different
supported decisions. Do not create a word-substitution menu.

| Candidate | Useful when | Must still explain |
|---|---|---|
| Offer-led: service/category + audience + practical use | Visitors do not know what is being sold | The specific help and next action |
| Outcome-led: useful result + mechanism | Visitors recognize the problem | What is actually delivered and relevant limits |
| Alternative-led: a better-supported way to do the task | The audience already uses an identifiable workaround | The concrete difference without disparaging unsupported competitors |

A headline need not contain every element. Let the supporting line carry mechanism,
audience or qualification that the headline cannot hold. Keep an essential limit
adjacent to its claim; do not rely on distant fine print to reverse a guarantee.

Choose a candidate using the supplied audience, visit intent, awareness, evidence
and channel space. Give a reason tied to those inputs and one tradeoff. If there is
no basis to choose, identify the competing hypotheses and propose a comprehension
test. Do not invent a numerical quality score to make a preference look measured.

## 3. Give each copy element a job

- **Opening:** let the intended reader recognize the offer and its relevance. A
  company story can support trust later when it answers a real customer question.
- **Supporting line:** explain the mechanism or deliverable that makes the opening
  credible. Prefer a concrete description over another aspiration.
- **Proof:** match the claim's scope. A sample demonstrates an output; a dated,
  permissioned case supports that customer's result; neither proves universal lift.
- **Path:** show customer work, provider work and important prerequisites. Separate
  “how to begin” from the longer delivery process when they differ.
- **Objections:** answer the questions that could block the next decision, using
  known facts. Unknown integration, pricing or eligibility terms remain open.
- **Action:** use a label consistent with the actual commitment. Distinguish a
  purchase, quote request, demonstration and information download. Where diagnosis
  or assessment is paid separately from later work, state that distinction beside
  the action; “approve repairs later” must not imply a free inspection.
- **Alternative action:** add one when a reader has a different useful readiness
  step. Explain its role rather than making unrelated actions equally prominent.

For broad offers, group by a distinction visitors can recognize: job, service need
or audience. Provide example labels and a route for ambiguous cases. An internal
business-unit name is not automatically a useful customer category. Choose the
primary offer from visitor intent and business context, not an arbitrary category
count or a universal revenue-first rule.

## 4. Diagnose before rewriting

| Symptom in the supplied copy | Diagnostic question | Revision move | Check the revision |
|---|---|---|---|
| Company history dominates | What does the visitor need to decide first? | Lead with offer/relevance; move relevant history to proof | Can the reader name the offer? |
| “Transform,” “empower,” “better” without specifics | What activity changes, through what capability? | Name activity and mechanism | Does the line distinguish this offer? |
| Feature list with no value connection | Which task does each feature support? | Connect selected features to supported outcomes | Is the benefit traceable to a capability? |
| Emotion or stakes feel manufactured | Where did this feeling or consequence come from? | Use evidenced language or a practical obstacle | Does the copy still explain why the task matters? |
| Proof is impressive but irrelevant | Which exact claim does this asset substantiate? | Relocate, qualify, replace or remove it | Can the claim and proof be read together? |
| Several equally urgent actions | What decision is this page meant to advance? | Select a primary action and purposeful secondary path | Is each commitment and consequence clear? |
| Simplified process conceals work | What happens after the first click? | Distinguish first step from delivery prerequisites | Are price, eligibility and effort represented where relevant? |
| Clear but interchangeable copy | Could a different offer use it unchanged? | Restore the supplied differentiator and mechanism | Is a meaningful difference visible without a superiority invention? |

For a requested revision, quote or identify the weak passage, explain the defect,
show replacement copy, and trace the change to an input. Do not stop at a checklist
or critique. Preserve supported useful material instead of rewriting everything.

## 5. Inspect the draft as a reader

Read the headline, supporting line, proof caption and action label together. Then
check the path and objections. Record whether the actual copy shows:

1. What is offered and for whom.
2. A useful customer outcome and the mechanism or deliverable supporting it.
3. The supplied meaningful difference, when one exists.
4. Proof relevant to consequential claims and limits still attached to them.
5. A next action with an intelligible consequence, including separate assessment
   charges or commitments where applicable.

For an author's desk review, cite the lines and missing evidence. For user testing,
ask representative readers to explain these points without coaching; record their
answers and misconceptions before revising. These are separate forms of evidence.

Complete when the selected copy, selection rationale, revision record and unresolved
inputs are in the message brief. Route approved brand expressions to `brand-designer`
and behavior questions to `product-design-and-ux`. If positioning is still unknown,
stop polishing unsupported differentiation and return that question to the owner.

The customer/offer/path/action structure and website grouping are drawn from the
