# Customer-centered messaging

Use this reference for website messaging, customer narratives, and unclear offers after positioning is established. This is a synthesis of common messaging practices, not a formal industry standard or a guaranteed conversion formula.

## When to use

Use after identifying an audience and a defensible offer, when website copy or other marketing material fails to explain why that offer matters or what to do next. Use the same message foundation across channels while adapting detail and tone.

Resolve unknown customer needs through `product-discovery`. Resolve alternatives and differentiation through `go-to-market` positioning. Brand identity and voice documentation belong to `brand-designer`; task behavior belongs to `product-design-and-ux`. Do not impose a sales narrative on support instructions or product workflows whose purpose is completing a task.

## Workflow

1. **Establish the decision context.** Identify the audience, purchase stage, goal, current alternative, relevant difference and actual offer. Separate users, buyers and approvers when their needs differ. Mark inputs as observed, supplied, hypothesized or unknown; do not invent interviews.
2. **Explain the obstacle.** Describe the practical problem and consequences in customer language. Include feelings or values only where evidence supports them. Do not manufacture insecurity, villains or moral stakes to complete a template.
3. **Define the business's useful role.** Explain the help provided and why the business can deliver it. Pair understanding with relevant proof: an attributable case, demonstrated capability or explicit delivery commitment. Treat unsupported metrics and testimonials as missing evidence.
4. **Describe a feasible path.** Show what a customer does, what the business does and how the customer reaches value. A short summary may link to procurement, migration or onboarding detail; it must not conceal consequential cost, effort or prerequisites. Step count follows the actual process.
5. **Make the decision concrete.** Give the main action an informative label and describe its consequence: request a quote, book a consultation, try a product or buy. If a lower-commitment option fits the audience, make its purpose distinct. Do not invent scarcity, availability or follow-up terms.
6. **Describe the outcome and stakes accurately.** Distinguish promised deliverables from hoped-for results and measured past outcomes. State proportionate consequences of leaving the problem unresolved where relevant. Do not imply that declining this vendor inevitably causes failure.
7. **Express the message through the established voice.** Draft a short offer statement and supporting explanation with proof, objections and next action. Preserve the distinctive value and vocabulary that the audience understands. Narrative is a coherence check, not a requirement for theatrical copy or seven visible page sections.
8. **Translate to the channel.** For a landing page, propose an offer/benefit opening, relevant proof, intelligible offer groups, process and action expectations. Order sections around the visitor's decision; no universal wireframe is established by this research. Give UX the expected action, resulting state, information needs and unresolved constraints.
9. **Test before claiming improvement.** Ask representative people to explain the offer, audience, value, evidence and next step in their own words. Record selection, prompts, observations and errors. A brief exposure can diagnose confusion; a fixed five-second threshold is not a universal pass rule. If running an authorized experiment, predefine the comparison, outcome, guardrails and stopping rule. Track qualified demand and subsequent outcomes as well as clicks.

Produce a `templates/customer-message-brief.md` with input evidence, a draft, claim/proof mapping, action expectations, channel handoff and an explicit validation state. Stop when the reviewable draft and unresolved questions are documented. Publication and claims of improved performance require their own evidence.

## Worked example — fictional, not customer evidence

Assume a supplied service: weekly cash-flow forecasting for independent shops; onboarding requires connecting accounting records and reviewing assumptions. No measured savings are available.

- Weak offer: “Our visionary platform empowers business transformation.”
- Candidate: “Plan next week's payments with a weekly cash-flow forecast for your shop.”
- Explanation: “Connect your accounting records, review the assumptions with us, and receive an updated forecast each week.”
- Action: “Request a walkthrough.” State who follows up and on what terms only after those facts are confirmed.
- Proof gap: request a sample forecast and permission to use relevant customer results. Do not invent savings, guaranteed solvency or testimonials.

The draft specifies a customer goal and service path while leaving unproven impact unclaimed. It is an illustration, not a tested message.

## Evidence boundary

This workflow is a practical synthesis, not a tested universal sequence. Public framework prescriptions and revenue anecdotes do not establish causal sales lift. Independent web-writing research supports clear, scannable, factual prose in a limited historical usability setting; it does not validate this entire workflow. For source attribution, locators, limitations and extraction records, see the repository [research provenance](../../docs/research/customer-centered-messaging/sources.md).
