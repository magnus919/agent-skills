# Canon, Evidence, and Creative Boundaries

## What this skill treats as its primary corpus

The core corpus is William Gibson's Sprawl fiction:

1. *Neuromancer* (Ace, 1984)
2. *Count Zero* (Arbor House, 1986)
3. *Mona Lisa Overdrive* (Bantam, 1988)
4. The linked stories in *Burning Chrome* (Arbor House, 1986), especially the stories set in the same fictional continuity

The official author site lists these works in Gibson's bibliography and identifies him as the writer credited with coining “cyberspace.” It is useful for bibliographic confirmation, not for detailed world claims: <https://williamgibsonbooks.com/> (accessed 2026-07-26).

For an exact claim about a named element, consult the relevant edition, note its edition and location, and describe only what the passage supports. Editions paginate differently, so chapter and scene context are often safer than a bare page number.

## What the corpus supports at a high level

The texts repeatedly stage:

- networked space as a contested economic and perceptual environment rather than a neutral information utility;
- power distributed among corporations, families, criminal intermediaries, security arrangements, and autonomous intelligences rather than centered in a civic state;
- people who survive through specialist knowledge, informal exchange, personal obligation, and access to systems they do not control;
- bodies as vulnerable, modified, commodified, and socially situated, not as clean upgrades;
- built environments that show use, maintenance, uneven access, and commercial saturation;
- global cultural movement without an uncomplicated single future culture.

These are recurring textual patterns, not a license to call every cyberpunk trope “Gibson canon.” The novels differ in focus and scale, and no single passage demonstrates every pattern.

## Three labels for outputs

| Label | Meaning | Example wording |
|---|---|---|
| **Textual observation** | Directly visible in a named primary text or supplied excerpt. | “In the supplied passage, access is mediated by a commercial intermediary.” |
| **Supported inference** | A careful conclusion from multiple observations or a cited critical source. | “This suggests a city where formal security and informal service routes coexist.” |
| **Original adaptation** | New work using the method, not canon. | “For an original setting, make the clinic a leaseholder’s after-hours repair cooperative.” |

Never turn an inference into a quotation, an original invention into canon, or a visual convention into evidence about a particular book.

## Common confusions to avoid

- **The Sprawl is not an aesthetic mood board.** It is a specific fictional setting with names, events, and relationships. Do not use them casually in an ostensibly original commercial artifact.
- **“Cyberpunk” is not a single lineage.** Japanese urban imagery, noir, punk, postmodernism, global finance, computing, and many later games and films have distinct histories. Do not attribute later genre inventions to Gibson without evidence.
- **Japanese or other diasporic presence is not decoration.** The fiction's cultural and economic crossings do not justify generic kanji, vague “Asian market” scenery, or invented multilingual text.
- **Corporate power is not omnipotence.** In this mode, institutions have limits, internal rivals, dependencies, and intermediaries. A good world model asks how power is exercised and evaded.

## Further contextual sources

Use these to frame or challenge an interpretation, not to replace close reading:

- William Gibson, interview with Larry McCaffery, “An Interview with William Gibson,” *Mississippi Review* 16, no. 2/3 (1988): 217–236. A contemporaneous primary interview about the work and its milieu.
- Dani Cavallaro, *Cyberpunk and Cyberculture: Science Fiction and the Work of William Gibson* (Athlone Press, 2000). A focused critical account; use as interpretation, not evidence of plot facts.
- David Brande, *The Business of Cyberpunk: Symbolic Economy and Ideology in William Gibson* (Westview Press, 1994). Useful for claims about economics and ideology, with its period and critical stance made explicit.
- N. Katherine Hayles, “The Life Cycle of Cyborgs: Writing the Posthuman,” in *The Cyborg Handbook* (Routledge, 1995). Useful for wider posthuman context; it is not a guide to the Sprawl canon.
