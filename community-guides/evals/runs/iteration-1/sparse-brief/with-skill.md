# Neighbors Together: a short discovery draft

**Status:** bounded discovery draft; not a finished local advice or safety guide.

## A welcoming starting point

Neighbors Together has a reason to begin a conversation about “the problem,” but
the problem itself has not been specified yet. This draft keeps the next step
small: learn what concern residents want help with, what already works, and what
would make a future guide usable.

Please do not treat this document as advice about a hazard, service, route, or
local requirement. No local authority, service, partner, or resident consensus
has been verified.

## What is known

- There is a group called **Neighbors Together**.
- The intended audience is described only as **neighbors**.
- The supplied brief is a deliberately sparse synthetic fixture for offline work.
- No place, topic, or hazard has been supplied.

## What is not known yet

These gaps materially change what a useful guide would say:

- What place, building, neighborhood, or shared community boundary is in scope.
- What “the problem” is, and whether it is an everyday issue, a preparedness
  concern, or something requiring specialist or emergency advice.
- Which neighbors need to be heard, including languages, disability access,
  connectivity, transport, cost, timing, or attendance constraints.
- What source material, existing practice, or trusted local information exists.
- Who owns and approves the guide, and who will maintain it.
- Which delivery channel is actually available: paper, an in-person conversation,
  phone, or an online channel.
- The time horizon and the smallest useful success measure.
- Whether the group wants a discovery conversation or a draft for review first.

## The smallest useful next step

Before writing topic-specific instructions, the organizer can offer residents a
short, voluntary discovery activity. It can be done in the channel the group
confirms is available. The activity should take only as long as people can spare;
written, spoken, drawn, or private responses should all be acceptable. Nobody
needs to provide a name, address, health information, or a private contact detail.

### Resident input card

**Purpose:** identify one useful guide topic and one feasible next action without
claiming that every neighbor has the same experience.

You may skip any question. Share only what you want the group to use.

1. **What concern would you most like a short neighbor guide to help with?**
   Use a few words, a drawing, or a spoken answer.
2. **What already helps, even a little?**
3. **What makes this difficult to act on?**
   Examples of answer types: time, cost, access, language, information, trust, or
   needing another person to agree. These are prompts, not assumptions about you.
4. **What would a useful first step look like for you?**
5. **What would make the guide easier to use?**
   For example: large print, another language, paper, a short conversation, or
   something else.
6. **How would we know the guide helped?**
   Name one observable change, or say “not sure yet.”

**Keep private:** anything identifying, sensitive, or about another person.

**May be shared only with permission:** a de-identified summary of the concern,
barrier, or suggested first step. Do not publish individual responses by default.

### A worked fictional response

> Concern: “I want a simple way to learn what neighbors think should happen
> first.”  
> Already helps: “People already check in informally.”  
> Barrier: “Some people cannot attend at the same time.”  
> Useful first step: “A short paper question that can be returned later.”  
> Success: “The group can name one shared priority and one next step that people
> have actually agreed to try.”

This example is fictional. It is not evidence about this community.

## Organizer notes for a safe discovery conversation

### Before inviting participation

1. Confirm the topic and geographic or membership boundary, or state that the
   activity is only scoping those questions.
2. Name the person or group responsible for receiving responses. If nobody has
   accepted that role, do not collect responses yet.
3. Choose an available response route and provide an offline alternative if the
   group needs one.
4. Explain whether responses will be summarized, who will see them, and how a
   participant can decline or withdraw a contribution.
5. Do not promise a service, volunteer role, purchase, referral, or outcome that
   has not been accepted and checked.

### During the activity

- Start by saying that this is discovery, not a test of a resident’s knowledge.
- Allow passing, private completion, partner assistance, interpretation, and
  follow-up when feasible.
- Ask for experiences and barriers without requiring traumatic stories or
  personal disclosure.
- Record competing needs as competing needs. Do not convert a few responses into
  consensus.
- If the topic turns out to require emergency or specialist guidance, pause the
  guide-writing process and obtain appropriate authoritative advice before giving
  instructions.

### Close and learn

Offer a short summary for correction:

| What we heard | How certain is it? | What needs checking? |
|---|---|---|
| Main concern, in residents’ words | Supplied by participating residents; not yet representative | Who was not reached and whether the wording is accurate |
| Existing help or practice | Resident report; not independently verified | Whether it is current, safe, accessible, and available to others |
| Main barrier | Report from participants; not a complete population finding | Whether the proposed first step fits people who were absent |

Ask: “What did we misunderstand?” and “What should we not record or share?”
Keep only the minimum information needed to make the next draft better.

## Decisions needed before a full guide

The organizer can answer these in a short planning conversation; residents should
not be asked to solve all of them.

1. What specific concern is in scope, and what is explicitly out of scope?
2. Who is the guide for, who is missing from that description, and how can those
   perspectives be reached without burden or pressure?
3. Which languages, formats, access arrangements, cost limits, connectivity, and
   timing must the guide support?
4. What information is already trusted or available, and what needs independent
   verification?
5. Who accepts ownership of the guide, receives corrections, and approves a
   shared action or role?
6. What is the smallest capability residents should be able to demonstrate after
   using it?
7. What would count as a useful result, and when should the guide be revisited?
8. Does Neighbors Together authorize a discovery conversation now, or should this
   draft first be reviewed by the organizer?
