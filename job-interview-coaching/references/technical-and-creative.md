# Technical, research, product, and creative professions

These are authored coaching adaptations. Microsoft illustrates a technical interview workflow; Oxford documents portfolio and academic formats. Neither supplies universal employer rubrics. Obtain the actual task and role criteria before making specialist judgments.

## Software, infrastructure, security, and data

For coding: clarify requirements, identify a workable approach, implement within permitted tools, check examples and edge cases, and discuss cost and limitations. Coach communication without substituting a solution before the candidate tries. Inspect code or test output before claiming correctness. A fluent explanation cannot prove working code.

For architecture: establish users, load, reliability needs, constraints, and failure scenarios; compare options and explain operational tradeoffs. Adjust complexity to the responsibility level. Do not make large-system terminology a prerequisite for an entry-level role.

For data and research: clarify the decision or hypothesis, data provenance, assumptions, leakage, uncertainty, baselines, and evaluation. Distinguish prediction, association, and causal claims. Ask what would change the conclusion and what was actually observed. A mock that lacks data cannot validate empirical results.

Synthetic prompts:

- Junior developer: “How would you check a function that groups orders by customer?”
- Senior platform specialist: “Describe a reliability improvement that had a meaningful cost tradeoff.”
- Data analyst: “A metric rose after a launch. What would you check before attributing the rise to the change?”

Interviewers: use role-relevant tasks, clarify assistance rules, and score requirements understanding, reasoning, execution, and verification separately. Do not pretend one algorithm puzzle covers an entire engineering role.

## Physical engineering and laboratory work

Probe requirements, calculations or models, tolerances, test methods, safety, standards, failure analysis, and review authority. Clarify personal responsibility for a design and who approved it. Use domain sources for correctness; do not certify a design from interview prose. A live machinery or lab demonstration requires authorized supervision and appropriate controls, outside a text mock's capability.

Prompt: “Describe a test result that contradicted your model. What changed in your design or investigation?” Strong evidence identifies the test, discrepancy, investigation, decision, and verification. Do not require a successful redesign if an honest negative finding was the right outcome.

## Product, design, and creative work

Use portfolio work to establish problem, audience, constraints, options, individual contribution, collaboration, iteration, feedback, and outcome. Distinguish aesthetic preference from task success. Where impact is unknown, say so; do not invent conversion improvements or user research.

For a product case, clarify the user and decision, compare choices, identify evidence and tradeoffs, and propose a proportionate next step. Do not force a memorized framework into an incompatible problem.

Prompt: “Show a project where feedback changed the direction. What did you own, and what did you choose not to change?” Interviewers should distinguish authorship from team output and avoid requesting confidential client work.

## Academic and research appointments

Separate teaching demonstration, research presentation, proposal defense, and panel conversation. Establish audience, discipline, resources, and expectations. Practise explaining contribution, methods, limitations, future work, funding feasibility where relevant, and teaching decisions. Do not generalize faculty expectations to a research assistant or technician.

Prompt: “What is the strongest alternative explanation for your result?” A coach can test clarity and reasoning; technical validity may require a discipline expert. Academic-job practice is distinct from student admissions, although some formats overlap.
