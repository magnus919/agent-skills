# People management, director, and executive interviews

## Basis and task selection

[Lou Adler's public accomplishment/problem discussion](https://www.louadlergroup.com/the-complete-2-question-interview/) supplies a practitioner example of connecting past execution with proposed judgment. This playbook's leadership scenarios and anchors are original; no proprietary scorecard is reproduced.

Establish actual mandate, authority, team/portfolio scope, stakeholders, time horizon, resources, and business or service context. A senior IC is not a people manager by default. Executive titles vary widely; ask what decisions the person would own and what the board or sponsor retains.

## Evidence to develop

Elicit enabling others, priorities, delegation, feedback, difficult people decisions, resource allocation, governance, and consequences. Probe what the leader personally decided and what the team did. Examine alternatives under the information available then, not hindsight certainty. Ask about setbacks and conflicting objectives, not only scale.

## Worked contrast

Original synthetic question: **Describe a decision that helped one organizational objective while making another harder. How did you decide and review it?**

Weak example: “I drove transformation across the company and delivered amazing growth.”

Stronger example using different, explicitly fictional facts: “I owned the department's capacity plan, not the whole enterprise budget. I proposed moving two people to a critical dependency, which delayed a local improvement. I discussed the competing outcomes with the other lead and the accountable sponsor, set a review point, and tracked whether the dependency improved. I would separate our contribution from wider market growth.”

The stronger fictional response identifies scope, tradeoff, authority, collaboration, and review. It may demonstrate useful cross-functional judgment without proving enterprise executive readiness. Do not inflate a department story into company-wide authority. Identify what additional scope the target role requires.

Use this contrast to explain evidence quality, not to put fictional achievements into the user's answer. For a real transcript, any improved autobiographical answer must use only supplied or subsequently confirmed facts.

## Neutral probes

- What did you delegate and how did you stay accountable?
- Who disagreed, and what evidence changed the discussion?
- Which outcome did not improve and what did you do about it?

## Candidate practice

Build a portfolio of decisions: priorities, people development, a failure, a conflict, and a change under uncertainty. Practise explaining each at board, peer, and team levels without altering facts. For executive preparation, rehearse questions about mandate, sponsor alignment, capital/people authority, and how success will be judged.

Transfer task: Change the available resources or remove a sponsor's agreement. Ask which decision rights and assumptions need clarification before carrying the same plan forward.

Keep the baseline, coached retry, and independent transfer attempt distinct. Ask one question at a time and wait. If the user wants a simulation, withhold coaching until the agreed debrief. If they want an explanation or plan, do not force a mock.

## Responsibility-level adaptations

First manager: delegation, feedback, team delivery. Director: cross-team resources and operating systems. Executive: enterprise mandate, governance, risk and stakeholder choices. Senior IC: technical influence and judgment; do not require direct reports or treat people management as the only path to seniority.

A title alone is insufficient. Ask what independence, complexity, impact, and decision authority the role actually requires. Early-career candidates may use relevant education, voluntary, caring, or project evidence; experienced candidates should explain the scope and limits of their contribution.

## Interviewer practice and candidate experience

Define the leadership job before scoring. Probe causal claims and decision rights rather than rewarding prestige, employer brand, headcount, or confidence alone. Use consistent questions and independent ratings. Treat candidate diligence as legitimate; a senior appointment needs truthful discussion of constraints and authority.

For interviewer coaching, rehearse the probes against both a vague and a concrete answer. Record what evidence the question elicited, what remains missing, and whether a hint supplied the response. A friendly conversation is not itself a complete assessment. Give candidates space to understand the work and ask informed questions.
