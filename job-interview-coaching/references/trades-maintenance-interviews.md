# Trades, maintenance, and manufacturing interviews

## Basis and task selection

[O*NET industrial-machinery mechanic record](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/49-9041.00) supplies occupational task context. [UK apprenticeship guidance](https://www.apprenticeships.gov.uk/apprentices/getting-an-apprenticeship) confirms varied interview formats and transferable experience. Neither supplies a technical safety answer key.

Establish the trade, plant/site setting, apprentice versus qualified scope, supervision, and whether the task is discussion, fault-finding, or practical demonstration. Clarify what is taught after hiring. A first-job applicant should not need an independent repair history when the role is explicitly an apprenticeship.

## Evidence to develop

Elicit methodical observation, following instructions with understanding, quality checks, identifying uncertainty, appropriate escalation, documentation, and handoff. For experienced practitioners, add nonroutine diagnosis and prevention. For production roles, discuss deviations, quality and teamwork without making speed the only success criterion.

## Worked contrast

Original synthetic question: **A fault appears again after a repair was marked complete. How would you investigate and communicate the problem within your role?**

Weak example: “I would try different fixes until it worked and keep production going.”

Stronger example using different, explicitly fictional facts: “I would establish what was observed, review the previous repair and checks, and stay within the task and authority I had been given. I would make sure the responsible person knew the fault had recurred and clarify what could be checked safely under the site procedure before further work. I would document what was found and how the result was verified.”

The stronger answer shows a structured investigation and boundaries, but its wording does not prove safe practical competence. Obtain the actual standard and domain reviewer before judging a technical sequence. Do not teach or improvise hazardous isolation or machinery-restart instructions from a generic mock.

Use this contrast to explain evidence quality, not to put fictional achievements into the user's answer. For a real transcript, any improved autobiographical answer must use only supplied or subsequently confirmed facts.

## Neutral probes

- What information would help distinguish recurrence from a new fault?
- Which check or decision was yours, and which required supervision?
- What evidence would you include in the handoff?

## Candidate practice

Use a real de-identified task: symptom, checks, decision, action within scope, verification, and handoff. For an apprentice, use a learning or instruction-following example from school, volunteering, or a project. On the next attempt, change the symptom and ask what information is needed, not a memorized repair.

Transfer task: Change the fault history or handoff information. Ask what assumption no longer holds and what evidence would justify proceeding.

Keep the baseline, coached retry, and independent transfer attempt distinct. Ask one question at a time and wait. If the user wants a simulation, withhold coaching until the agreed debrief. If they want an explanation or plan, do not force a mock.

## Responsibility-level adaptations

Apprentice: learning, reliability, recognition of limits. Qualified practitioner: independent checks and routine diagnosis. Senior specialist: nonroutine faults and tradeoffs. Supervisor: planning, quality, training, workload, and safe escalation arrangements.

A title alone is insufficient. Ask what independence, complexity, impact, and decision authority the role actually requires. Early-career candidates may use relevant education, voluntary, caring, or project evidence; experienced candidates should explain the scope and limits of their contribution.

## Interviewer practice and candidate experience

Test entry requirements, not skills promised in post-hire training. Use a safe, bounded demonstration only with appropriate supervision. Agree technical criteria with the trade specialist. Avoid leading prompts that reward automatic agreement with the supervisor, and do not confuse lack of corporate vocabulary with lack of practical judgment.

For interviewer coaching, rehearse the probes against both a vague and a concrete answer. Record what evidence the question elicited, what remains missing, and whether a hint supplied the response. A friendly conversation is not itself a complete assessment. Give candidates space to understand the work and ask informed questions.
