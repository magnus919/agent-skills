# Candidate coaching

## Diagnose before prescribing

Ask for a baseline answer or use the supplied transcript. Identify the dominant difficulty: missing experience, weak retrieval, poor example selection, unclear reasoning, unfamiliar format, or communication under pressure. Do not treat a knowledge gap as a wording problem. If the user lacks an example, explore adjacent truthful experience or acknowledge the gap and explain how they would learn.

Agree a useful outcome: demonstrate a capability, answer more directly, explain a decision, or evaluate a role. Do not substitute “sound confident” for a substantive goal. Adapt directness to the user's request while keeping criticism about behavior and evidence.

## Build an evidence bank

Create a small set of versatile examples rather than one script for every possible question. Capture:

- Capability and relevance to the target role.
- Context and stakes, including constraints.
- Personal responsibility, authority, collaborators, and shared credit.
- Actions and reasoning, alternatives considered, and why the choice made sense then.
- Results and how they were observed; distinguish attribution from coincidence.
- Learning, changes made afterward, and limits on generalization.
- Confidential details that need redaction.

School, caring, voluntary work, informal work, and projects can supply evidence. Match the expectations to the role. Apprentices are not expected to demonstrate the same autonomy as licensed practitioners. Do not stretch one modest example into a false leadership claim.

## Build an answer, not a performance persona

For a behavioral question, STAR or CAR can organize a relevant incident. Keep context sufficient to understand the decision; give actions and reasoning enough space. No universal percentage allocation is justified. A result can be qualitative. Reflection should explain learning, not append “communication is important.”

For motivation: connect actual interests, relevant experience, and specific features of the work. For “tell me about yourself,” select a short role-relevant narrative and invite follow-up; do not recite an entire CV. For a weakness: choose a real, relevant limitation, its effects, and what the user is doing about it. Avoid disguised strengths and invented redemption stories.

For conflict or failure: distinguish facts, personal contribution, others' perspectives, repair, and learning. Do not sanitize away responsibility or encourage disclosure of protected customer or employer information.

When rewriting, preserve factual boundaries and the user's voice. Mark missing facts as questions or placeholders. Ask the user to attempt the revised answer before concluding that the weakness is resolved.

## Candidate diligence

Interviews also help candidates decide whether to accept the work. Adapt questions to what matters:

| Context | Useful question |
|---|---|
| First job | How are new starters trained and who helps when they get stuck? |
| Shift or frontline work | What are typical scheduling expectations and how are changes handled? |
| Trade or regulated work | What supervision, equipment, and escalation arrangements support safe work? |
| Specialist role | Which decisions would I own and which need review? |
| Management | What outcomes am I accountable for and what resources can I change? |
| Executive | Where do the board, sponsor, and executive disagree about the mandate? |

Questions should obtain information, not merely signal sophistication. Do not invent company facts. Treat concerns as diligence questions until there is supporting evidence.

## Completion

Deliver a role-specific evidence map, answer practice, or preparation brief as requested. End with the highest-value next exercise and what it tests. When the interview is imminent, prioritize logistics, a few strong examples, candidate questions, and one short rehearsal instead of a large curriculum.
