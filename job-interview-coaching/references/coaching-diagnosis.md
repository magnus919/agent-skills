# Diagnose the bottleneck and choose the intervention

Use after an initial answer or transcript review, especially when generic advice has not helped. These are coaching hypotheses, not diagnoses of personality or mental health. Confirm them through a small task; one weak answer rarely identifies its cause.

| Observable pattern | Competing explanations | Discriminating check | Intervention and transfer check |
|---|---|---|---|
| General claims without an incident | Retrieval difficulty, misunderstood question, little relevant experience | Ask for a real occasion using a familiar context | Build an incident timeline; later ask a differently worded question using another event |
| Long background, little action | Fear of missing context, uncertain relevance, no clear decision | Ask for a one-sentence answer to the actual question | Practise answer-first opening; reintroduce only context needed by the listener |
| “We” throughout | Genuine team ownership or unclear individual role | Ask which choice or task was personally theirs | Map responsibilities; do not erase collaborators or demand heroic solo action |
| Detailed action without outcome | No follow-up data, omitted result, or unsuccessful outcome | Ask what was observed and what is unknown | State verified results and limits; practise a negative-result example honestly |
| Correct terminology, weak reasoning | Rehearsed knowledge or unstated reasoning | Change one constraint and ask what changes | Work on concepts or decision logic; polish wording only after understanding improves |
| Strong untimed answer, freezes in mock | Format unfamiliarity, cognitive load, language demands, access barriers | Offer thinking time or a written attempt | Gradually rehearse relevant conditions; do not infer a clinical cause |
| Excellent coached retry, weak new answer | Script dependence or narrow learning | Ask a new question without the outline | Reduce assistance and test retrieval/transfer before advancing |
| Candidate disagrees with feedback | Coach error, missing context, preference, or misunderstanding | Return to the excerpt and ask what was missed | Correct unsupported criticism; retain evidence-supported feedback with explanation |
| Interviewer gets shallow answers repeatedly | Poor questions, little probe time, mismatched level | Rewrite one question and rehearse a neutral probe | Repair elicitation; do not conclude every candidate is weak |

## Intake that earns its cost

Use the supplied goal and artifacts. A transcript already contains a baseline; do not require another generic mock first. If the user asks to start practice, begin promptly using a reasonable stated role assumption. Ask about the job or timing only when it changes the next exercise. Access preferences can be offered without requiring a diagnosis.

Establish the immediate goal in observable terms: “explain one personal decision and its result,” “test a proposed solution,” or “ask an evidence-seeking follow-up.” “Be more executive” or “show confidence” needs translation into role-specific behavior before it becomes a useful target.

## Evidence inventory and story selection

Record the actual event, approximate period, stakes, authority, collaborators, actions, alternatives, result, source of the result, and later learning. Add which capabilities it can support and what it cannot prove. Reusing a genuine event across questions is reasonable when the selected facts answer each question. Repeating a fixed script regardless of the question is not.

Distinguish four shortages:

1. **Evidence exists but is not retrieved.** Reconstruct a real event using sequence, people, records, and decisions. Do not ask the user to invent a more dramatic incident.
2. **Evidence is adjacent.** Explain what transfers from volunteering, caring, education, or another field and what remains untested.
3. **Evidence contradicts the claim.** Correct the claim or select a different example rather than hide the contradiction.
4. **Evidence does not exist.** State the gap and prepare a credible learning or hypothetical response if the question permits it. A hypothetical is not a past achievement.

Select a balanced bank: solving a problem, collaboration or conflict, learning, a setback, integrity or safe boundaries, and role-specific craft. Do not force everyone to produce revenue, direct reports, or a major crisis. Difficulty and scope should match the entry requirements.

## Feedback as a small experiment

State the hypothesis: “The decision is hard to identify because the answer spends its opening on context.” Agree the test: another attempt with the decision in the first sentence. Compare what changed, then ask a new question to see whether the behavior transfers. If relevance was already clear, the hypothesis was wrong; do not continue shortening merely to satisfy a formula.

Offer one or two interventions at a time. Feedback should identify the observed behavior, its consequence for this task, an actionable change, and how to test it. Do not reward confidence without evidence, or punish thoughtful qualification that the task requires.

After two or three unproductive retries, change the exercise, reduce the load, seek missing facts, or stop with a specific next action. This is a default coaching bound, not a validated threshold. The user can choose to continue.

## Selecting frameworks

STAR/CAR helps organize a particular past event. Add reasoning or learning when the question requires it. A present-to-future opener helps orient the listener to a career narrative. A hypothesis–evidence–recommendation structure suits many cases. A requirements–approach–test structure suits technical tasks. The framework should make the reasoning visible; its labels are not scored capabilities.

Before a framework change, ask whether the issue is actually missing content. A polished STAR answer with no personal action still lacks evidence. A technically sound concise explanation should not be downgraded because it does not name the framework.
