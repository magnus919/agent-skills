# Trades, frontline service, logistics, and administration

Authored adaptations grounded in general structured interviewing, public apprenticeship guidance, and employer hospitality examples. Obtain local role instructions and domain standards for technical correctness. Do not assume all occupations use an office-style interview or have quantified commercial outcomes.

## Trades, maintenance, manufacturing, and field service

Elicit reliability, diagnostic process, workmanship, checks, safe boundaries, documentation, and handoff. For apprentices, emphasize learning and recognizing when to seek supervision. For experienced practitioners, probe independent decisions and nonroutine faults. For supervisors, add planning, quality, training, and escalation.

Synthetic question: “A repair seems complete, but the fault returns on the next shift. Walk me through what you would check and communicate.” Neutral probes: what is known, what needs verification, what is within your authority, and how would you establish a safe handoff?

Do not prescribe electrical isolation, machinery recovery, or other hazardous procedures from generic coaching knowledge. Ask for the applicable standard and use qualified review. A text conversation can assess explanation; it cannot prove hands-on competence or grant certification.

Candidate diligence: supervision, tools, training, shift expectations, stop-work authority, and who reviews completed work. Avoid implying that unsafe improvisation demonstrates initiative.

## Retail, hospitality, customer support, and personal services

Use examples of customer needs, prioritization, service recovery, coordination, boundaries, accuracy, and asking for help. Avoid rewarding “the customer is always right” when safety, policy, or other customers' needs matter. First-job examples may come from school, clubs, volunteering, or caring.

Synthetic prompt: “One customer needs help with a mistake while two others are waiting. What would you do first?” Assess reasoning against the stated scenario and authority, not a secret ideal script. Neutral probe: “What would make you involve a colleague or supervisor?”

Candidates can ask about training, scheduling, peak periods, escalation, and support. Availability should be handled consistently in relation to actual job requirements, without probing private family circumstances.

## Logistics, transport, warehouse, and operations

Probe accuracy, checks, handoffs, workload, deviations, incident reporting, and decisions under time pressure. Do not equate speed with performance when it compromises safety or quality. Use role-specific evidence: delivery discrepancy resolved, inventory error traced, safe escalation, or a reliable changeover.

Prompt: “A shipment discrepancy is discovered near a deadline. What information would you verify before deciding the next action?” The coach should not invent transport rules, license requirements, or safety standards.

## Administration and coordination

Probe prioritization, accuracy, confidentiality, scheduling dependencies, communication, and maintaining records. A useful exercise might be triaging a synthetic inbox or identifying a conflict in a schedule. Use fictional data and clarify permitted tools.

Prompt: “Two colleagues give you conflicting urgent requests. How would you establish priorities and communicate the decision?” For senior coordination roles, add cross-team dependencies and improvement of recurring processes.

## Interviewer cautions

Use bounded, safe demonstrations when job-relevant. Specify training assumptions and avoid testing skills normally taught after joining as though they were entry requirements. Do not penalize lack of corporate vocabulary. Practical demonstration design needs an appropriate domain owner; a generic question bank is insufficient.
