# Prepare by question type

Use for question-specific preparation. The structures and examples below are original coaching suggestions; employer instructions override generic conventions. Examples are fictional and should not become the user's biography.

## Opening narrative and motivation

For “tell me about yourself,” give the listener a role-relevant map: current or recent work, the experience most relevant here, and why this next role makes sense. First-job candidates can begin with what they have been learning and doing. Senior candidates should explain their actual scope, not inflate it through a title.

Weak: “I was born in… then I studied… then I worked…” Stronger fictional outline: “I coordinate volunteer rotas, where I learned to resolve coverage gaps. I am applying for this scheduler role because accurate handovers and helping people are the parts I want to develop.” The next probe should test the coverage-gap evidence, not congratulate a polished opener as proof of skill.

For “why us?”, connect genuine interests to verified features of the work and unresolved questions. Separate the organization, the role, and the user's timing. If the employer's claims are unverified, phrase them as things to explore. Do not invent lifelong passion or tell every candidate that mission agreement proves fit.

## Behavioral and accomplishment questions

Choose an event that actually answers the requested capability. Establish situation and task briefly, personal decisions/actions, outcome and evidence, and learning where relevant. Ask what others contributed. If the interviewer asks about a failure, an entirely successful project with a token obstacle will not answer it.

Weak fictional response: “I improved communication.” Stronger response using supplied facts: “I noticed the handover omitted unresolved bookings, so I proposed a shared pending-items list. My colleague maintained it on the next shift; we could then see which customers still needed a callback. We did not measure waiting time.” This shows contribution without invented business impact.

## Weakness, mistake, conflict, and termination

A weakness answer should identify a real limitation, how it affects work, steps taken, and evidence of progress or remaining difficulty. Do not use a disguised strength or coach disclosure of a severe personal matter merely to sound authentic.

For a mistake, distinguish detection, ownership, containment, communication, repair, and prevention. If no remedy was possible, explain what was learned without fabricating recovery. For conflict, represent the other person's legitimate concerns and the decision process; disagreement is not proof that someone was difficult.

For job loss or a gap, give a truthful concise account within the user's disclosure boundaries, then connect to readiness. Do not demand health or family detail. If facts are disputed, help the user describe their perspective accurately rather than assert an unsupported legal conclusion.

## Hypothetical and technical questions

A hypothetical asks what the candidate would do. Establish missing information, priorities, actions within authority, and contingencies. Label assumptions. Do not turn the scenario into an invented past event.

A technical question may require an actual solution. Clarify requirements, demonstrate the approach, verify it, and examine limitations. Communication feedback cannot substitute for checking correctness. If a candidate is stuck, use a graduated hint only in coached mode and record it; a simulation probe must not quietly supply the solution.

## Case, portfolio, presentation, and teaching

Cases need a decision and analysis; portfolios need authorship and design choices; presentations need audience, purpose, evidence, and synthesis; teaching needs a learning goal and a way to check understanding. Do not give all four the same STAR script. Use the relevant professional playbook and invitation.

For a cancelled project, report what was learned or changed before cancellation. For shared work, identify the user's contribution. For missing data, state what the evidence can support. “Impact” does not always mean money or an increased percentage.

## Candidate questions and closing

Develop questions that resolve actual decisions: expectations, supervision, resources, schedule, safety, ownership, how success is assessed, and what is changing. Match the person's authority. A trainee may need training and supervision details; an executive may need board alignment and mandate boundaries.

At closing, summarize interest accurately and ask about unresolved process details if useful. Avoid pressure tactics and promises the user cannot make. Follow-up messages should be factual, brief, and unsent unless authorized. They can clarify an omitted fact but should not become a fictional second interview.

## Unexpected and inappropriate questions

Ask for clarification when a question is ambiguous. If it is intrusive, the user can redirect to the work requirement, ask its relevance, decline the private detail, or raise the concern with the appropriate contact. Offer options rather than a one-size script. Current jurisdiction and context are necessary before declaring a specific question unlawful.

## Delivery without conformity

Practise comprehensibility, organization, pace when observable, and handling interruption. Do not impose a universally correct accent, personality, gesture, or answer duration. Offer a shorter opening plus optional depth, rather than cutting the reasoning needed for the task. Written practice is valid; text alone cannot establish how the user sounded or looked.
