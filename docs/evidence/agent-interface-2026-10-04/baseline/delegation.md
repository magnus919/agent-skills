# Billing correction across two services

Design the flow around the customer’s intended outcome and the authority they granted, including the recipient-side work performed by each service. First clarify the correction’s scope, affected billing period, maximum amount or adjustment boundary, eligible accounts, and whether the customer authorizes each service to commit independently. These decisions belong to the product and billing owners. Until approved, show the proposal and identify unresolved fields; do not infer broad authority from a general request.

The customer reviews a single understandable summary: account identity, current charge, proposed correction, affected services, expected timing, and what cannot be rolled back. Each service’s action is listed separately with its own status and consequence. A preview step validates account, amount, policy, and current state. The agent may gather information and draft the correction. Human approval is required for the exact final operations if policy says the change is consequential; approval binds to the displayed scope, target accounts, amounts, service versions, and expiry. Any material change invalidates the approval and returns to review. The customer should be able to reject, edit within permitted bounds, or escalate to a person.

Represent the distributed task as a parent operation with child operations, each having a stable ID and status: proposed, awaiting approval, authorized, pending, committed, failed, cancelled, compensation pending, compensated, or outcome unknown. Before acting, persist the approved intent and child IDs. Invoke each service with a scoped idempotency key where supported. Record acknowledged operation IDs and reconcile service state before retrying after timeouts. Do not promise an atomic transaction across independent services. In this case, once service A commits and authorization is revoked while service B remains pending, immediately block new work on B, cancel it if cancellation is supported, and reconcile whether it raced to commit. Revocation governs future actions but does not erase A’s completed effect.

Tell the customer plainly that the correction is partial: service A applied it, service B is stopped or still being reconciled, and the total billing outcome is not complete. Show amounts and dates only when confirmed by the services, and separate observed facts from pending status. Offer the allowed next steps: leave the partial correction in place, request an authorized compensating action for A, or resume B after a fresh scoped authorization. Compensation is a new consequential action and requires its own approval unless an explicit pre-authorized policy covers it. Never silently undo A or continue B after revocation.

Recovery evidence includes per-service request and operation IDs, authorization version and revocation time, observed effects, status timestamps, and reconciliation results. A timeout or lost acknowledgment yields “unknown” until queried. The UI must support interruption and re-entry without duplicate commits, stale approval, or false success. Completion requires both services’ verified terminal outcomes matching the approved plan, or a clearly accepted partial-resolution outcome if the customer chooses it. Test revocation before either commit, between commits, while the second service is in flight, and after both commit; also test duplicate delivery, cancellation failure, and unavailable status APIs. Audit and retain only necessary data under the assigned owner and retention policy.
