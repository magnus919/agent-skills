# Steve Yegge on harness engineering and multi-agent coordination

Research through November 25, 2026. [Source records](sources.md) · [Research and media log](research-log.md) · [Catalog assessment and experiments](catalog-application.md)

## Research question

Which patterns survived Yegge’s progression from AI-assisted editing to long-running agent organizations, which did his operating experience qualify or reverse, and where would those changes improve the agent-skills catalog?

## Thesis

**Yegge’s most durable insight is to make work survive the model session. His most important later insight is to make the resulting organization governable at the rate humans can use its output.** Beads gave work identity, dependency and history. Gas Town added a way to claim work, wake workers, hand off and integrate. Wheelhouse exposed the next constraints: nobody could absorb the output, every correction could become standing policy, and a seat could spend inference and human attention while accomplishing nothing. Counting House then supplied an explicit second shape. For recurring, narrowly defined cases, Yegge proposed fresh workers in a deterministic workflow with item-specific evidence and authority. In his latest located interview he also described a modest enterprise entry point: a project-coordination agent that observes, asks, documents and recommends while a human owns the project. The synthesis is a portfolio of work shapes sharing durable records, effect controls and independent acceptance. ## Five findings

### 1. Build a durable work record before adding coordination machinery

Beads solved the practical problem that the process reading a conversation today may be gone tomorrow. The useful interface is a stable work ID, accepted intent and why, owner and current claim, dependencies, evidence, separate delivery and acceptance, and enough information for someone else to continue. The execution loop can discover the next ready item from the record. Treat wake delivery as a recoverable event. Keep the approved current view distinct from historical decisions and the agent’s working notes. Beads and Dolt implement one version of this design. The catalog can use an existing issue tracker and a small amount of runtime state when those systems together provide the needed semantics. ### 2. Carry roles across restarts when the work actually needs continuity

The Charlotte talk, Counting House essay and November DevCon keynote provide the clearest qualifier to the seat idea. Recurring design judgment, a product relationship, or a continuing decision backlog may justify a named seat with a concise remit, inbox and retrieval map. High-volume cases with a stable SOP and little inter-case dependency can use isolated sessions with an intent-specific policy row. A single sequential job may need only an ordinary harness. Consult authorized current policy at the point of action even when a returning seat can cheaply recover its previous context. The seat’s name, predecessor notes and model output are inputs to work. Effective authority comes from the host. ### 3. Curate policy as carefully as code

In August, Yegge made the case for outcome-specific enforcement at shared surfaces. In September, he reported that accumulated rulings and denials left no legal path for ordinary work. A simplification pass reopened work and removed a guard that should have held a release freeze. These are two failure modes of the same system. Protect mandatory controls and use authorized, versioned maintenance to consolidate redundant workflow rules. Exercise an example of legitimate work that should still pass and a consequential action that should still be denied. Keep drafts, expired exceptions and one-off feedback out of the live policy index. Yegge’s reports motivate the failure scenarios. His estimate of rules retained is a case detail. [S11](sources.md#s11) · [S12](sources.md#s12) · [S13](sources.md#s13) · [S16](sources.md#s16)

### 4. Design around the cost of accepted outcomes

The scarce unit can be reviewer time, player playtesting, communication attention, a shared build queue, inference or the ability to recover from a bad release. A gate that admits work when builders are idle will miss all of those. Count spend and labor across wake-ups, context loading, retries, independent review, integration and support. Pace human-facing releases and messages to actual capacity, with an escalation route for urgent exceptions. Preserve a release decision distinct from merge. An activity increase can be useful as a diagnostic, while an output count has little to say about accepted value. Google’s controlled configuration study and Anthropic’s parallel research system support workload-sensitive comparisons. Their measured gains and losses belong to their own settings. ### 5. Start by improving a decision loop

Yegge’s September advice to start with one production agent on a bounded queue and his November recommendation to start with supervised project coordination are compatible. One improves a repeatable service loop. The other exposes decisions and dependencies while leaving implementation and commitments with their owners. The November interview is clearest about a human being accountable for the project and pacing communications. Our catalog should start with a named sponsor, an authorized input set, one compact decision or dependency record, and a human review of the resulting work. Counting House adds the next step for casework that will eventually earn more autonomy: evaluate per intent on held-out real cases, use domain adjudication, weight errors by consequence and put transactions behind a separately controlled effect boundary. ## Confidence and open questions

| Assessment | Evidence and limits |
|---|---|
| **High confidence in the documented evolution.** | The essays, two November keynotes and November interview converge on the chronology. Wheelhouse reports across those formats mostly come from one person and one application. |
| **High confidence that topology needs a task-fit test.** | A controlled comparison found both gains and substantial losses from adding agents. An independent failure taxonomy and a vendor deployment report reinforce the need to measure verification and coordination cost. Specific gains are workload- and model-dependent. [X01](sources.md#x01) · [X02](sources.md#x02) · [X04](sources.md#x04) |
| **Moderate confidence in the proposed catalog changes.** | Durable role contracts, policy curation, workload-specific autonomy and bounded communication are concrete enough to test against existing guidance. The catalog already covers several of them in principle. The proposed experiments would establish the incremental value. [Catalog assessment](catalog-application.md) |
| **Open on the strongest performance and market claims.** | No comparable fleet run, enterprise case dataset or causal estimate established Wheelhouse’s economics, optimal rule count, anthropomorphic prompting benefit, 40% quality-work allocation, general superiority of smaller swarms, or forecasts about adoption, SaaS and employment. Treat respectful, specific feedback and readable designs as operational practices. Any causal effect of seat naming, tone or accumulated trust needs controlled evidence. |

The highest-value unanswered question for the catalog is whether a compact, current role packet improves accepted outcomes or cost under real handoff conditions, after controlling for model, tools, permissions and task mix. The most consequential failure test is whether a policy simplification preserves stop, release and authorization controls on every effect path while restoring useful work.
