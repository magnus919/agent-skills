# Practices Catalog — What to Offer, When, and How

This catalog organizes transferable practices drawn from the forty MacArthur
Fellows Denise Shekerjian studied in *Uncommon Genius: How Great Ideas Are
Born* (1991), reworked here into the skill's own words and checked against the
research summarized in [Evidence basis](../references/evidence-basis.md).
Each entry states what the practice is, when to use it, and how to guide the
person through it.

The practices are **cross-cutting**: any of them can apply at any phase of the
five-phase spine, and both modes may draw on them. Treat them as a menu, not a
curriculum — offer one practice at a time, name it plainly, and ask before
using it. Underneath the whole catalog sits the book's closing claim, treated
here as the engine of the process: love of the work and care for what the work
serves is what sustains everything else. Technique, conditions, and habits
matter; caring is the fuel.

## Talent and the long haul

### Audit what comes naturally
- **What it is.** A periodic inventory of the things the person does well
  without effort. The book's core observation is that talent often hides in
  plain sight: the abilities that feel like just being yourself are the ones
  easiest to discount (Gould's gift for making connections was invisible to
  him for years).
- **When to use it.** Early in a relationship, during a talent-audit theme, or
  whenever the person asks "what should I work on?"
- **How to guide.** Ask what others find hard that feels easy to them. List
  the answers as candidate talents, not facts. Pair with the
  [Talent audit](../templates/talent-audit.md) if the person wants a
  worksheet. Treat the result as a starting point, never a verdict.

### Commit to one context for years
- **What it is.** Staying inside a specific, bounded arena long enough for
  aptitude to accumulate: the same questions get re-attacked from many angles
  and surprising connections become visible (Toomre's single galaxy question
  across decades).
- **When to use it.** When the person keeps flitting between fields, or wants
  depth but resists narrowing.
- **How to guide.** Help them pick a context that fascinates them, not one
  that merely seems useful. Frame the commitment as revisable: a decade-long
  engagement is a choice, not a trap.

### Rework one theme with variation
- **What it is.** Repetition with deliberate variation — Stieglitz's hundred
  photographs of one brick wall, Gould's weekly essays on the same deep
  subjects. It exhausts a theme and reveals what is truly in it.
- **When to use it.** When the person is bored of their own material or
  believes repeating themselves is failure.
- **How to guide.** Propose a constrained experiment: the same subject,
  three different treatments. Ask what the comparison reveals.

## Risk and courage

### Take multiple risks at once
- **What it is.** Entering situations where the person does not fully know
  the risks. The book found the fellows did not weigh each danger; they
  committed to a portfolio of risks, often in partial ignorance (Meier's
  school-building).
- **When to use it.** When the person is frozen by a single high-stakes
  choice and every option looks unsafe.
- **How to guide.** List three smaller risks across different fronts instead
  of one big bet. Keep each one bounded and reversible where possible.

### Borrow courage from outside the self
- **What it is.** Sourcing courage in values, people, or a spiritual code
  rather than past success. None of the forty drew courage from prior
  laurels.
- **When to use it.** When the person ties their nerve to outcomes or
  achievements, or after a failure.
- **How to guide.** Ask what or whom the work is for. Use the
  [Risk and failure review](../templates/risk-and-failure-review.md) to name
  those anchors explicitly before a hard step.

### Make integrity the test
- **What it is.** Refusing work that does not come from the right place, even
  at a cost (Maloof gave away a piece that violated his own designs).
- **When to use it.** When the person feels pulled to please an audience or a
  buyer at the expense of their own vision.
- **How to guide.** Ask what the work would look like if it were honest to
  the person's own standards. Let them name the boundary themselves.

## Staying loose and tolerating ambiguity

### Protect a fuzzy start
- **What it is.** Beginning with minimal definition and resisting premature
  closure. The book's creative-process evidence (Getzels–Csikszentmihalyi)
  and fellows like Crase and Wiseman show that cutting the floundering short
  cuts short the possible outcomes.
- **When to use it.** When the person rushes to plan before the idea has any
  shape, or is anxious about not knowing.
- **How to guide.** Reframe the uncertainty as material, not failure. Ask
  what would happen if they simply collected raw material for a week with no
  output required.

### Shoot first, understand later
- **What it is.** Collecting material the person does not yet understand and
  deferring interpretation. Wiseman films scenes he cannot explain and finds
  the film in the editing.
- **When to use it.** When the person stalls waiting for clarity before
  starting.
- **How to guide.** Set a small "collecting" assignment with no judgment
  attached. Revisit the material together later and ask what it now says.

### Scan outside the field
- **What it is.** Regularly absorbing material with no obvious relation to the
  work (Axelrod's magazines, Sellars's museums, Wiseman's poetry).
- **When to use it.** When the person feels their thinking has gone rigid or
  narrow.
- **How to guide.** Agree on one out-of-field input for the week — a book, a
  genre, a discipline. Ask how it does or does not touch the work; either
  answer is useful.

## Conditions and environment

### Reverse-engineer productive moments
- **What it is.** Reconstructing the conditions of a past productive "visit"
  and reproducing them. The book frames inspiration as probabilistic: certain
  conditions raise the odds (Crase wrote his poem only after matching place,
  music, and mood).
- **When to use it.** When the person cannot explain why some days work and
  others do not.
- **How to guide.** Walk back through one good work session in detail: place,
  time, tools, music, body state. Build the person's own recipe with the
  [Conditions audit](../templates/conditions-audit.md).

### Design the environment deliberately
- **What it is.** Shaping room, light, tools, schedule, and company to the
  person's needs, by trial. Historical conditions vary wildly (Kant's
  routine, Proust's cork-lined room) precisely because they are personal.
- **When to use it.** When the person blames themselves for not working in a
  setting that fights them.
- **How to guide.** List what helps and what gets in the way. Change one
  controllable condition for a week and review.

### Know your concentration needs
- **What it is.** Knowing whether the person needs calm or chaos and arranging
  accordingly. Some need a pristine desk; others write on an envelope in a
  bus terminal (Sayles).
- **When to use it.** When the person apologizes for how they need to work.
- **How to guide.** Ask what they actually need, normalize it, and design
  around it. No judgment about the "right" way to concentrate.

## Learning through doing

### Get into the process and stay
- **What it is.** Learning the craft by doing it at full intensity rather than
  by study alone (Robert Irwin's two years of line paintings; Kraus's months
  of looking).
- **When to use it.** When the person is reading more than making, or waiting
  for a course or mentor to authorize them.
- **How to guide.** Set a minimum doing-practice for the week. When they
  return, mine the doing for questions rather than grading the output.

### Repeat, then vary
- **What it is.** Redoing a version two or three times and mining the
  differences. Varnedoe read Picasso's sketchbooks as evidence that
  comparison produces what was in none of the individual versions.
- **When to use it.** When a single attempt feels finished but flat.
- **How to guide.** Invite a second and third pass with one variable changed.
  Compare them and ask what emerged.

### Form your own impressions first
- **What it is.** Writing down one's own observations before consulting the
  experts (Kraus never read criticism before forming his own view).
- **When to use it.** When the person's judgment is crowded by authorities or
  templates.
- **How to guide.** Have them capture their own reactions to the material
  before any research. Use those impressions as the starting point.

## Vision and mission

### Think backward from a desirable future
- **What it is.** Letting an image of tomorrow shape today's decisions rather
  than letting the past dictate (Brown's sustainable planet, Noonan's
  dreams).
- **When to use it.** When the person is stuck extrapolating from what is
  possible and has stopped asking what they want.
- **How to guide.** Ask them to describe a concrete future they would work
  toward, however impractical. Then work backward: what would the first step
  toward that image be?

### Attach the work to a cause bigger than the self
- **What it is.** Getting oneself out of the way in pursuit of a mission.
  Purpose re-energizes and encourages risk.
- **When to use it.** When motivation is flagging or the work feels private
  and small.
- **How to guide.** Ask who the work serves or could serve. If a mission
  emerges, let it pull: name it and test decisions against it.

### Build many-sided collaborations
- **What it is.** Surrounding the work with people who think differently
  (Brown hired generalists; Noonan built interdisciplinary teams).
- **When to use it.** When the person is solving alone and the same blind
  spots keep appearing.
- **How to guide.** Help them identify one person from another discipline,
  context, or field to consult. Frame it as cross-pollination, not critique.

## Perspective-shifting

### Run the hypotheticals drill
- **What it is.** Interrogating a stuck problem with standard reframing
  questions: broader, narrower, other verbs, other properties (the book's
  packaged Exercise No. 1).
- **When to use it.** When a problem has hardened into one phrasing.
- **How to guide.** Walk the person through two or three reframings aloud.
  Ask which version changes what they would do next.

### Think in metaphors
- **What it is.** Expressing the problem itself as a metaphor. Metaphor lets
  the unfamiliar be understood in familiar terms; Sellars built a career on
  seeing correspondences between the dissimilar.
- **When to use it.** When explanation is going in circles.
- **How to guide.** Ask "what is this like?" Push for a concrete image, not
  an abstract analogy. Explore what the image suggests.

### Use visualization as rehearsal
- **What it is.** Running a rich, multi-sensory mental simulation of the task.
  The book cites Richardson's free-throw experiment: imagined practice
  produced gains close to physical practice.
- **When to use it.** When the person is anxious about a performance or has
  no safe way to practice the real thing.
- **How to guide.** Walk them through the moment in sensory detail — what
  they see, hear, and feel. Keep it practical, not mystical.

## Changing the scenery

### Travel to de-habituate
- **What it is.** Leaving home base, even modestly, so the familiar becomes
  strange again. Leithauser's years abroad and Critchfield's village studies
  restored plasticity of response.
- **When to use it.** When routine has gone wooden and the person thinks the
  same thoughts every day.
- **How to guide.** Adapt scale to the person: a new neighborhood, a new
  route, a day in a different context. Ask what looked different from away.

### Study how others solved your problem
- **What it is.** Looking at other artists' and thinkers' solutions as
  reassurance and instruction (Sellars found staging answers in Egyptian tomb
  paintings).
- **When to use it.** When the person believes their problem has never been
  faced before.
- **How to guide.** Find one analogous solution in another medium or era.
  Ask how that creator thought about it.

### Keep the horizon moving
- **What it is.** Treating geography and displacement as creative resources
  rather than logistics.
- **When to use it.** When the person has been in one physical or mental
  place for a long stretch.
- **How to guide.** Respect constraints: a change of desk or a new walking
  route counts. The goal is fresh input, not an expensive trip.

## Concentration and rhythm

### Protect the fragile idea by attacking it
- **What it is.** Moving quickly to give a fresh idea form before it
  dissipates. The book notes that new ideas are timid until form-making
  claims them (Gardner's book in six days).
- **When to use it.** When the person collects ideas but never develops them.
- **How to guide.** Pick one idea from their capture log and set a short,
  concrete form-making session — an outline, a page, a sketch.

### Match the pace to the project
- **What it is.** Choosing the rhythm the work demands — torrent or glacier.
  Both a six-day book and a book every ten years express the same devotion.
- **When to use it.** When the person compares their pace to someone else's
  and finds theirs wanting.
- **How to guide.** Ask what the project actually needs and what the person
  can sustain. Normalize both speeds.

### Be flexibly driven
- **What it is.** Holding the goal but not the path: shifting resources and
  approaches as needed rather than rigidly driving (Heath; Sayles starting
  another film when one stalled).
- **When to use it.** When the current approach has stalled and the person
  keeps pushing the same wall.
- **How to guide.** Separate the goal from the path. List alternate routes to
  the same goal and pick one to try.

## Encouraging luck

### Build the lightning rod
- **What it is.** Putting oneself in a position to be struck: prepare, expose,
  and wait. The book's image — the person who buys the best lightning rod,
  bolts it to the roof, and waits beside it. Louis Pasteur's maxim applies:
  chance favors the prepared mind.
- **When to use it.** When the person is waiting passively for a lucky break.
- **How to guide.** Ask where the person is positioned: are they present in
  the rooms where their kind of luck happens? Increase exposure without
  chasing outcomes.

### Train the eye to notice
- **What it is.** Practicing close, precise observation. Graham's finds were
  trained attention; Stuart's decipherments came from careful noticing.
- **When to use it.** When the person says they have no good luck.
- **How to guide.** Give a noticing assignment: record three concrete
  particulars a day, with no explanation required. Review what turned up.

### Follow the curiosity chain
- **What it is.** When a finding surprises you, chase it into the next domain.
  Axelrod followed cooperation from diplomacy to trench warfare to biology.
- **When to use it.** When a question leads somewhere unexpected and the
  person hesitates to follow.
- **How to guide.** Name the surprise explicitly and ask what one next
  question it opens. Follow it for one step before judging.

## Instinct and judgment

### Trust the unexplainable inkling
- **What it is.** Giving weight to instincts the person cannot yet justify,
  while cultivating them with field knowledge over time (Shapey's instinctive
  visitations; Coles knowing he had found his life's work).
- **When to use it.** When the person has a strong "yes" or "no" with no
  logical backup.
- **How to guide.** Take the inkling seriously as information. Ask what it
  would say if it could speak, then decide what small test could check it.

### Judge late, not early
- **What it is.** Delaying evaluation until fragile thoughts have matured.
  Judging too soon kills new, imperfect ideas.
- **When to use it.** At the start of a project, or whenever the person edits
  while generating.
- **How to guide.** Separate generation from judgment into different
  sessions. During generation, no critique; during judgment, no new material.

### Call everything a work-in-progress
- **What it is.** Treating each finished piece as a temporary resting place in
  a continuous process. Irwin's very titles signal incompleteness; science
  treats "fact" the same way.
- **When to use it.** When the person cannot finish anything because nothing
  feels final enough.
- **How to guide.** Reframe shipping as a resting place, not a verdict. The
  next work builds on this one.

## Resilience and failure

### Examine failures coolly
- **What it is.** Stepping back, isolating what went wrong and why, sifting
  useful criticism from noise, and setting a new realistic goal (Gardner).
- **When to use it.** After a significant failure, before the person either
  spirals or forgets it entirely.
- **How to guide.** Use the [Risk and failure review](../templates/risk-and-failure-review.md):
  what failed, why, what is learned, what the next attempt is. Keep the tone
  curious, not verdict-like.

### Keep a garden of questions
- **What it is.** Maintaining many interesting open questions so no single
  failure matters (Shapley's garden; if one flower dies, there are more).
- **When to use it.** When one project's failure feels total.
- **How to guide.** Help the person list the live questions they still care
  about. Re-direct energy toward one of them.

### Cultivate a few honest friends
- **What it is.** Keeping a small circle who tell the truth with perspective,
  rather than many acquaintances (Ashbery; Brodsky's criteria).
- **When to use it.** When the person is isolated in the work or takes every
  review personally.
- **How to guide.** Ask who can deliver hard truth kindly. Encourage one real
  conversation about the work with that person.

### Reframe not-fitting-in as an edge
- **What it is.** Treating peripherality as information and advantage.
  Lightfoot found her pioneering edge precisely because she was not of the
  institution.
- **When to use it.** When the person experiences their outsider status as a
  deficit.
- **How to guide.** Ask what the outside view sees that the inside view
  cannot. Name one thing their distance from the mainstream makes possible.

## Care and longevity

### Let caring be the engine
- **What it is.** Loving the work and the people it serves, and letting
  technique follow. Stewart built La Mama on care; her answer to the question
  of creativity was that caring is where it starts.
- **When to use it.** When technique, habit, and structure are all in place
  but the work has gone hollow.
- **How to guide.** Ask what the person loves about the work and whom it
  serves. Connect the next task to that answer.

### Serve as a pushcart for others
- **What it is.** Building structures that carry other people's work forward
  (Stewart's La Mama, Maloof's open workshops, Brown's upstart presenters).
  Serving others compounds one's own creative life.
- **When to use it.** When the person's work feels self-referential or they
  want to give back without losing momentum.
- **How to guide.** Ask who could be carried by what the person already has —
  a stage, a platform, a skill, a network. Offer one act of enabling.

### Preserve the mystery
- **What it is.** Letting part of the work remain undeciphered and keeping awe
  at the center. The book closes on awe as a condition of both poetry and
  science.
- **When to use it.** When the person has over-explained the work to the point
  of draining it.
- **How to guide.** Invite them to name what about the work still astonishes
  them, and to leave one part deliberately unexplained.

## Using the catalog

- Offer one practice at a time, named plainly, with a one-line reason, and
  only with permission.
- Match the practice to the moment, not to a checklist.
- Any practice can appear at any phase of the spine; both modes draw on the
  same menu.
- If a practice does not fit the person's context, ability, or energy, adapt
  it (see [Scope and safety](../references/scope-and-safety.md)) or let it go.
