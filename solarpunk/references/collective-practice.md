# Collective practice: who holds the future?

## Choose a real shared need

Start with what people want to improve and who is already working on it. A repair service,
shared kitchen, cooperative workspace, accessible transport group, energy association,
seed library, or neighborhood cooling room can all be useful. A project does not need to
use the word solarpunk to offer a transferable mechanism.

Design Justice centers affected people's participation and community control. ICA defines
cooperative identity around democratic member control and shared ownership. These are
adjacent resources, not proof that a particular organizational form always succeeds.
[S06, S19]

## Establish a workable charter

Identify purpose, affected people, membership, decision authority, resources, roles,
conflict process, accessible participation, maintenance and exit. Separate ownership,
operational responsibility, funding and the right to use a service: these need not belong
to the same people. Specify how people can join, decline, challenge a decision, and leave.

For a pilot, make authority limited and reviewable. Sociocratic consent is one option:
seek reasoned objections within an agreed domain rather than require everyone's favorite
proposal. It is not a synonym for silence, unanimity, legal consent or a complete governance
system. Make participation meaningful and provide a way to raise concerns privately. [S20]

## Participation is part of the work

Budget for translation, disability access, childcare or care constraints where relevant,
travel, different schedules, and compensation for expertise. Do not assume a free evening
meeting represents everyone. Invite participation only through authorized channels; a
research request does not authorize collecting names or contacting residents.

Recognize local and Indigenous knowledge on its holders' terms. Consult the relevant
communities and authoritative local sources rather than treat decolonial language as a
license to extract designs. UNDRIP is an important reading lead; an excerpt about consent
is not a jurisdiction-specific legal analysis. [S21]

## Funding and ongoing care

Estimate start-up resources and continuing costs separately. Record rent/tenure, insurance,
consumables, utilities, tools, replacement, training, access support and coordination.
Cooperative ownership does not remove scarcity or business risk. Compare membership fees,
public support, earned revenue, donations and grants without inventing eligibility.
Avoid a model that makes poor members provide all labor while affluent members receive benefits.

Give essential recurring work an owner, backup, capacity limit and means of support.
Include a handover and closure pathway: what happens to funds, data, tools and commitments
if volunteers leave? Check local rules before forming entities or handling pooled funds.

## Evaluate public benefit

Use a few measures connected to the need: services actually delivered, access and exclusion,
affordability, maintenance completion, participant burden, complaints and resolution.
Environmental estimates need a baseline and method. Attendance is evidence of attendance,
not necessarily empowerment, emissions savings, or lasting trust.

Compare keeping the pilot, changing it, joining an existing service, or stopping. Listen
to the people who did not participate and to harms as well as success stories. Do not
scale a photogenic project before its operating model and community support work.

## Institutions and advocacy

Solarpunk can include public infrastructure, labor, housing policy, commons and civic
participation as well as small DIY projects. Map who can make a change and what evidence
would persuade them. A draft policy brief can be produced without sending it. Explain
political options as options; follow the user's framing and do not assume an organizing mandate.
