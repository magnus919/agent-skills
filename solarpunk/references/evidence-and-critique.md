# Evidence and critique

## Use different evidence for different questions

| Claim type | Appropriate evidence | Insufficient substitute |
|---|---|---|
| A text advocates a value | The text and context | A claim that the value works everywhere |
| A visual communicates an idea | Inspection and audience interpretation | Assumed engineering performance |
| A project exists | Dated primary record, current verification when needed | An unattributed image |
| A project improves outcomes | Baseline, method, results, limitations and ideally independent analysis | Promotional copy or existence alone |
| A proposal is feasible here | Site/context evidence, authority, resources, qualified assessment | A similar project elsewhere |
| A practice is legally allowed | Current applicable authoritative rules | Another country's guidance |

Keep distinctions in ordinary prose rather than label every creative sentence. Readers
need to know what matters for their decision, not a ceremonial evidence checklist.

## Research route

For substantial investigation, formulate a decision question, inclusion/exclusion criteria,
search terms and source diversity; record access dates, provenance and uncertainty. Use
primary movement texts for interpretation; official/local and research sources for material
claims; practitioner accounts for methods and case leads. Account for shared origins when
sources repeat each other. A manifesto and a derivative essay are not independent outcome evidence.

Search in local languages where possible and retain original names. Translation is not
proof of cultural authority. Favor affected communities' own records alongside official
accounts. Avoid treating a handful of English sources as global coverage.

For every retained source, extract the actual relevant claim, what supports it, what it
cannot establish, and how it affects the output. Preserve this with the artifact rather
than leave only a link list. Record blocked or rejected sources. If full text is unavailable,
use only the visible excerpt and say so. Never invent an access date or imply a whole
article was read when it was not.

## Greenwashing and other failure modes

Test a proposal by asking:

- **Baseline:** compared with keeping/repairing the current system, what improves?
- **Boundary:** which imports, emissions, waste, costs or harms are outside the frame?
- **Power:** who owns, controls, can contest, and can afford access?
- **Labor:** who maintains it and with what support, pay and working conditions?
- **Ecology:** are habitat, water, land and material effects credible in this place?
- **Life:** is it enjoyable, accessible, useful and culturally meaningful to inhabitants?
- **Evidence:** which results are measured and which are aspirations or estimates?

A speculative image can succeed as art without an engineering dossier. A developer's
carbon-neutral housing claim needs much stronger evidence. Scale scrutiny to the claim.

## Common corrections

**“Panels mean off-grid.”** Check controls, storage, charging and seasonal service.
**“Plants mean biodiversity.”** Check species, habitat relationships and maintenance.
**“Reuse is always best.”** Compare condition, safety, usefulness and lifecycle alternatives.
**“Local means equitable.”** Check access, ownership, exclusion and effects elsewhere.
**“No conflict means utopia.”** Look for whose disagreement has been omitted.
**“All technology is liberation.”** Examine extraction, surveillance, dependency and repairability.
**“Real solarpunk requires this politics.”** Explain the strand and respect the user's framing.
**“An impressive project proves the whole future.”** Transfer mechanisms conditionally.

## A constructive critique format

State the intended benefit and strongest feature; identify the consequential unsupported
claim or excluded perspective; explain its practical consequence; propose a revision and
the evidence needed. Do not substitute contempt for analysis or assert fraud without evidence.
Offer multiple valid futures when the disagreement is about values rather than facts.

## Stopping rule

Stop when the decision-relevant questions have adequate evidence or clearly identified
limits. If sources are blocked or local facts missing, supply bounded findings and the
next retrieval/measurement required. A long bibliography is not a reason to claim completeness.
