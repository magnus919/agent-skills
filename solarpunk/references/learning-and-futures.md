# Learning, futures and play

These facilitation formats are authored practice proposals informed by the skill's
creative and participatory orientation. They have not been validated as educational or
community-intervention outcomes.

## Match the activity to a purpose

| Purpose | Activity | Useful artifact |
|---|---|---|
| Introduce solarpunk | Compare two future scenes and ask who lives/works there | Shared vocabulary and open disagreements |
| Build imagination | Describe an ordinary day in a locally desirable future | Short scene, drawing, object, soundscape or map |
| Connect vision to action | Work backward from one desired service | Dependencies and a reversible first step |
| Learn practical skills | Repair/making/growing activity with appropriate supervision | A useful object and explanation of limits |
| Explore institutions | Roleplay a community resource decision | Multiple options and consequences |
| Critique greenwashing | Compare promotional claims with available evidence | Claim/evidence/unknowns table |

Use age-appropriate material, accessible formats, and local language. Participation may
be spoken, drawn, tactile, written or asynchronous. No one must disclose private hardship
or agree with the facilitator's preferred politics.

## Example 75-minute community imagination session

- **10 minutes:** establish purpose, access preferences and what decisions the session can
  actually influence. Record perspectives without claiming the group represents everyone.
- **15 minutes:** map assets and difficulties already present. Include services, relationships,
  skills, joy and care rather than listing only deficits.
- **20 minutes:** small groups depict a future ordinary day; ask how a person with different
  resources or access needs experiences it. Welcome different futures rather than force consensus.
- **20 minutes:** choose one desired change and work backward: who has authority, what
  exists, what dependencies matter, and what could be tried with permission?
- **10 minutes:** offer voluntary next steps, record disagreement and unknowns, and decide
  who may share the artifacts. A workshop does not itself authorize a project.

Adjust timing rather than sacrifice translation or access. Budget appropriately for any
materials, venue, facilitation, care and follow-up. Use a separate safety plan for tools,
food, children or site visits where applicable.

## Backcasting without prophecy

Choose a future condition in plain terms: reliable accessible trips, comfortable homes,
affordable energy, clean water, secure tenure, or time for creativity. Compare several
paths rather than claim history must converge on one system. For each path, map enabling
institutions, resources, skills, incentives and contested decisions. Separate steps the
participants control from steps requiring partners or political change.

A horizon can be this week, five years, or a fictional century. Long horizons invite
imagination; immediate horizons require actual authority and feasible resources. Identify
signals that would justify changing the path. Avoid presenting speculative futures as forecasts.

## Games and experiential artifacts

A tabletop game can place players in roles with different information and needs. Make
tradeoffs visible and allow cooperation without requiring one solution. Include upkeep,
access and delayed consequences; do not reward hiding burdens outside the map.
A design-fiction artifact—a repair receipt, ferry timetable, community notice, garment
label—can make a world concrete while remaining explicitly fictional.

## Evaluate honestly

Check the stated learning goal with an explanation, practical demonstration, reflection,
or useful artifact. Collect optional feedback about inclusion and burden. Enjoyment and
attendance are valuable but do not demonstrate changed behavior, political agreement,
community consent, or environmental improvement. Keep participant data minimal and obtain
permission before publishing identifiable contributions.
